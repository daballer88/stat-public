Medicine runs on lab shorthand. A single order can read *CBC, CMP, INR and troponin*, and the report that comes back is a column of letters, numbers and units. Knowing what the letters stand for makes most of it readable.

This guide decodes the abbreviations you will meet most often: what each one stands for, which panel it belongs to, what it mainly looks at and its units. What a high or low value means is covered in our guide to [reading lab results](/learn/reading-lab-results/).

## Key points

- Many lab abbreviations name a panel: a basic metabolic panel (BMP) is eight tests, and a comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP) is the same eight plus six more covering blood proteins and the liver.
- Most US laboratories report conventional units such as mg/dL, while most other countries use SI units such as mmol/L, so the same sample can give very different-looking numbers: read the unit first.
- Some results hide unit traps: CRP can be reported in mg/L or mg/dL, a factor of 10 apart, and troponin in ng/mL or ng/L, a factor of 1,000.
- The INR turns the prothrombin time into a ratio so results can be compared between labs, which matters for people taking warfarin.

## Panels, single tests and units

Many abbreviations name a **panel**, a group of tests ordered together under one name. A basic metabolic panel, for example, is eight tests. Other tests, such as troponin or lipase, are usually ordered on their own.

Units are the other half of the code. Most US laboratories report **conventional units**, which mostly give the mass of a substance in a set volume of blood, such as milligrams per deciliter (mg/dL). Most other countries use **SI units** (from the French *Système International*), which give the amount of substance in moles per liter, usually millimoles (mmol/L) or micromoles (µmol/L). The same sample can give two very different-looking numbers, so read the unit first.

## Complete blood count (CBC)

The **CBC** counts the cells in the blood and describes the red cells: how big they are and how much hemoglobin they carry. A **CBC with differential** ("diff") also sorts the white cells into their five main types.

| Abbreviation | Stands for | Mainly looks at | US units |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| WBC | White blood cell count | Cells that fight infection | cells/µL |
| Diff | Differential: neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils | Which white cells are affected | % and cells/µL |
| RBC | Red blood cell count | Oxygen-carrying cells | million/µL |
| Hb, Hgb | Hemoglobin | The protein that carries oxygen | g/dL |
| Hct | Hematocrit | Share of the blood made up of red cells | % |
| MCV | Mean corpuscular volume | Average red cell size | fL |
| MCH, MCHC | Mean corpuscular hemoglobin, and its concentration | Hemoglobin in each red cell | pg; g/dL |
| RDW | Red cell distribution width | Variation in red cell size | % |
| Plt | Platelet count | Cell fragments that help blood clot | /µL |

SI reports give counts per liter instead of per microliter. A liter holds a million microliters, so a white cell count of 7,500/µL becomes 7.5 × 10⁹/L, and 250,000 platelets/µL becomes 250 × 10⁹/L. Hemoglobin in SI is given in g/L, ten times the g/dL figure.

## Metabolic panels: BMP and CMP

The **BMP** (basic metabolic panel) has eight tests: four electrolytes, two kidney tests, glucose and calcium. It is also called a chemistry panel or *Chem-7*. The **CMP** (comprehensive metabolic panel) is the same eight plus six more, which cover blood proteins and the liver.

| Abbreviation | Stands for | Mainly looks at | US units | SI units |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Na | Sodium | Fluid and electrolyte balance | mEq/L | mmol/L (same number) |
| K | Potassium | Electrolyte balance | mEq/L | mmol/L (same number) |
| Cl | Chloride | Electrolyte balance | mEq/L | mmol/L (same number) |
| CO2, HCO3 | Carbon dioxide, mostly as bicarbonate | Acid–base balance | mEq/L | mmol/L (same number) |
| BUN | Blood urea nitrogen | Kidney function | mg/dL | mmol/L (× 0.357) |
| Cr | Creatinine | Kidney function | mg/dL | µmol/L (× 88.4) |
| Glu | Glucose | Blood sugar | mg/dL | mmol/L (÷ 18) |
| Ca | Calcium | A key mineral for nerves and muscles | mg/dL | mmol/L (÷ 4) |

The CMP adds:

| Abbreviation | Stands for | Mainly looks at | US units | SI units |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alb | Albumin | A protein made by the liver | g/dL | g/L (× 10) |
| TP | Total protein | All the protein in the blood | g/dL | g/L (× 10) |
| ALP | Alkaline phosphatase | The liver | U/L | U/L |
| ALT | Alanine aminotransferase (old name SGPT) | The liver | U/L | U/L |
| AST | Aspartate aminotransferase (old name SGOT) | The liver | U/L | U/L |
| T bili | Total bilirubin | A waste pigment the liver handles | mg/dL | µmol/L (× 17.1) |

The figure in parentheses turns a US value into SI: a creatinine of 1.0 mg/dL is about 88 µmol/L, and a glucose of 90 mg/dL is 5.0 mmol/L. Sodium, potassium, chloride and bicarbonate keep the same number because each ion carries a single charge, so one milliequivalent is one millimole. Enzymes such as ALT are measured by their activity in units per liter (U/L); the strict SI unit, microkatals per liter (µkat/L), appears on some reports.

## Liver tests (LFTs)

**LFTs** (liver function tests) is a loose term, not one fixed panel. It usually means the liver tests already on a CMP (ALT, AST, ALP, bilirubin and albumin), often ordered as a **hepatic function panel** that also reports **direct bilirubin** alongside the total. **GGT** (gamma-glutamyl transferase), another liver enzyme reported in U/L, is a common addition. The **PT/INR** is often read with them, because the liver makes prothrombin, one of the clotting proteins. For what the pattern of liver results suggests, see [reading lab results](/learn/reading-lab-results/).

## Clotting tests: PT/INR, aPTT and D-dimer

| Abbreviation | Stands for | Mainly looks at | Units |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PT | Prothrombin time | How long a blood sample takes to clot | Seconds |
| INR | International normalized ratio | The PT, standardized so labs can be compared | None (a ratio) |
| aPTT, PTT | Activated partial thromboplastin time | Clotting time through a different set of clotting factors | Seconds |
| D-dimer | A protein fragment, not an abbreviation | The breakdown of blood clots | Often µg/mL or ng/mL |

The **INR** exists because raw PT results vary with the lab and the testing method. Turning them into a ratio makes results comparable, which matters for people taking the blood thinner warfarin, whose dose is adjusted using the INR. The aPTT is commonly used to monitor heparin, another blood thinner. **D-dimer** is a known unit trap: labs report it in several different units, so a value means little without the unit printed beside it.

## Arterial blood gas (ABG)

An **ABG** (arterial blood gas) uses blood from an artery, often at the wrist, to measure oxygen, carbon dioxide and the blood's acidity. A **VBG** (venous blood gas) uses blood from a vein instead.

| Abbreviation | Stands for | Mainly looks at | US units | SI units |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| pH | Acidity scale | Acid–base balance | None | None |
| PaO2 | Partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood | Oxygen in the blood | mm Hg | kPa (÷ 7.5) |
| PaCO2 | Partial pressure of carbon dioxide in arterial blood | Carbon dioxide in the blood | mm Hg | kPa (÷ 7.5) |
| HCO3 | Bicarbonate | Acid–base balance | mEq/L | mmol/L (same number) |
| SaO2 | Arterial oxygen saturation | Share of hemoglobin carrying oxygen | % | % |

The small *a* stands for arterial. **SpO2**, with a *p* for pulse oximetry, is the bedside estimate described in our guide to [vital signs](/learn/vital-signs/). Pressures convert at about 7.5 mm Hg per kPa, so 90 mm Hg is 12 kPa.

## Inflammation, heart and pancreas markers

These are usually ordered singly, not as part of a panel.

| Abbreviation | Stands for | Mainly looks at | Common units |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CRP | C-reactive protein | Inflammation | mg/L or mg/dL |
| hs-CRP | High-sensitivity CRP | Cardiovascular risk | mg/L |
| ESR | Erythrocyte sedimentation rate ("sed rate") | Inflammation, indirectly | mm/hr |
| cTnI, cTnT | Cardiac troponin I or T | Heart muscle injury | ng/mL, or ng/L for high-sensitivity tests |
| BNP | B-type natriuretic peptide | Heart failure | pg/mL |
| NT-proBNP | N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide | Heart failure | pg/mL |
| Lipase | A digestive enzyme, not an abbreviation | The pancreas | U/L |

Two unit traps sit in this table. CRP can be reported in mg/L or mg/dL, a factor of 10 apart: 10 mg/L is 1.0 mg/dL. Troponin differs by a factor of 1,000: **high-sensitivity troponin** (hs-cTn) is reported in nanograms per liter and older tests in nanograms per milliliter, so 0.05 ng/mL equals 50 ng/L. BNP and NT-proBNP are related molecules with different cutoffs, so their values cannot be swapped.

## Diabetes and thyroid tests

| Abbreviation | Stands for | Mainly looks at | Units |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| HbA1c, A1C | Hemoglobin A1c (glycated hemoglobin) | Average blood glucose over the past 2–3 months | % in the US; mmol/mol in SI |
| TSH | Thyroid-stimulating hormone | The pituitary signal that drives the thyroid | mU/L, the same number as µU/mL |
| FT4 | Free thyroxine (free T4) | The main hormone the thyroid makes | ng/dL; pmol/L in SI |

**HbA1c** is the clearest case of two scales. US labs report a percentage, while international consensus asks labs to report SI units in millimoles per mole alongside the percentage. The two scales are linked by a fixed equation, so a conversion table or calculator is the easiest way to switch between them.

## Labs in Stat!

In Syndrome, the **Labs** tab reports each result as a word rather than a number. A test comes back **high**, **low** or **normal**; qualitative tests such as cultures and antibody tests read **positive** or **negative**, and descriptive ones such as a urinalysis or blood smear read **normal** or **abnormal**. There are no units to convert, so the skill is knowing what each test reports on: liver enzymes point to the liver, an INR to clotting and a troponin to the heart.
